Section 0 — Before You Begin: Two Worlds, One Choice

Imagine two kinds of builders.

Builder A works inside a mountain. Nobody ever sees what she builds — it's buried in sealed rock, invisible forever once it's done. But inside that rock, she has laid out millions of tiny tunnels, gates, and switches so precisely that electricity flowing through them can add numbers, remember values, and make decisions — all in billionths of a second. She doesn't get to "plug in a wire and see a light blink." Her feedback loop is slow: she designs for months, simulates for months more, and only after all of that does she send her design off to a factory (a "fab") that costs billions of dollars to build a physical chip. If she made a mistake, she often can't fix it — she has to wait for the next chip. This is Digital VLSI (Very-Large-Scale Integration) — the art of designing the silicon itself.

Builder B works above ground, with things you can hold. He takes a chip that Builder A (or a company like her) already built — a microcontroller — and wires it to motors, sensors, screens, and radios. He writes software that lives directly on the metal of that chip, with no operating system holding his hand. His feedback loop is fast: he changes one line of code, flashes it to the board in ten seconds, and sees a motor spin or an LED blink immediately. This is Embedded Systems — the art of making silicon do something in the physical world.

Here is the honest, uncomfortable truth about choosing between them:

Digital VLSIEmbedded Systems
Feedback loopSlow — simulation-only for years before real siliconFast — seconds to minutes, physical results
What you're closest toPhysics, transistors, timingCode, protocols, real-world devices
Entry barrier (India)High — top VLSI roles often need M.TechLower — a strong B.Tech portfolio can get you in
Number of jobsFewer, concentrated in hub citiesFar more, spread across many industries
Tool costExtremely expensive EDA licenses (lakhs–crores)Cheap — a ₹500 ESP32 board is a full lab
What a 2nd-year can DIYSimulate for free (EDA Playground, iverilog); real chips stay out of reachActually build, wire, and ship real working devices
What "mastery" looks likeDeep physics + timing closure across huge designsDeep real-time constraints + system integration
Why this vault is built the way it is: Sections 1–2 teach the physics underlying chips and how to actually design digital logic in Verilog — the VLSI side, fully doable in free simulators. Sections 3–5 teach bare-metal register-level programming and real protocols — the embedded side, fully doable on your own ESP32. Section 6 shows where the two worlds are fusing right now (hardware built specifically to run neural networks). Section 8 gives an honest career and salary picture for India.

The test that actually tells you the answer: after finishing a Verilog FSM exercise in Section 2 and a bare-metal GPIO driver exercise in Section 3, ask yourself one question — "Which one did I stay up late doing for fun, not because it was assigned?" That answer is more reliable than any comparison table above. Keep a small note as you move through this vault: which sections make you lean forward, and which make you check the clock.

1 · Physical & Mathematical Foundations

Every digital chip you will ever touch — a RISC-V core, an ESP32, an Nvidia GPU — is, underneath everything, just an enormous number of tiny electrically-controlled switches arranged to implement Boolean logic. Before you can understand anything in VLSI or embedded systems, you need to understand these switches, how fast they can flip, and how numbers actually live inside them as voltages.

1.1 Transistor Physics to Logic Gates

The switch, explained like you're holding it in your hand. A MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is a water tap. Think of it literally as a tap over a pipe:

  • The Gate terminal is the tap handle — you turn it with a voltage, not your hand.
  • The Source and Drain are the two ends of the pipe — current can flow between them.
  • The Body/Substrate is the material the pipe is carved into.

There are two flavors, and they behave like opposite taps:

  • NMOS: the tap opens (conducts) when the gate voltage is HIGH. Good at connecting a node to ground (0V).
  • PMOS: the tap opens (conducts) when the gate voltage is LOW. Good at connecting a node to the supply (Vdd).

Why CMOS won the world. CMOS (Complementary MOS) pairs one PMOS and one NMOS for every gate. For any stable input, exactly one of the two conducts and the other is off — current never flows straight from Vdd to ground during steady state, only for the brief instant while switching. This is why CMOS chips run cool and can be crammed with billions of transistors.

The CMOS Inverter — the atomic unit of all digital logic

        Vdd (e.g. 1.2V)
         |
        ═╪═   PMOS  (conducts when IN = LOW)
         |
   IN ───┤
         |
        ═╪═   NMOS  (conducts when IN = HIGH)
         |
        GND

   OUT is tapped from the node between the two transistors (the middle wire).
  • When IN = 0 (LOW): PMOS conducts, NMOS is off → OUT pulled to Vdd → OUT = 1.
  • When IN = 1 (HIGH): NMOS conducts, PMOS is off → OUT pulled to GND → OUT = 0.

That single circuit is a NOT gate — the fundamental block from which NAND, NOR, AND, OR, XOR, flip-flops, adders, and eventually entire CPUs are built, purely by wiring more PMOS/NMOS transistors in series and parallel.

1.2 Power Equations — Why Your Phone Gets Hot

Dynamic Power — power consumed while switching:

\[ P_{dynamic} = \alpha \cdot C \cdot V^2 \cdot f \]

  • \(\alpha\) — activity factor: the fraction of clock cycles where this node actually switches (0 to 1).
  • \(C\) — capacitance being charged/discharged (bigger wires/transistors = bigger C).
  • \(V\) — supply voltage, squared. Dropping voltage from 1.2V to 0.9V cuts dynamic power by more than 40%.
  • \(f\) — clock frequency, directly proportional. Double the clock, double the dynamic power.

Static (Leakage) Power — power consumed even at rest, because real transistors are never a perfect open switch when "off":

\[ P_{static} = I_{leakage} \cdot V \]

At older nodes (90nm) leakage was negligible. At modern nodes (5nm, 3nm), transistors are so small electrons "tunnel" through gate insulation only a few atoms thick — leakage has become a massive fraction of total power.

Total Power:

\[ P_{total} = P_{dynamic} + P_{static} \]

1.3 Timing Analysis — The Heartbeat of Every Digital Circuit

Every synchronous circuit is built from flip-flops (memory elements capturing a value on a clock edge) connected by combinational logic (gates computing in between). Data must arrive at the next flip-flop with enough margin before and after the clock edge.

Setup Time (\(t_{setup}\)): data must be stable for at least this long before the clock edge, or the flip-flop may capture garbage.

Hold Time (\(t_{hold}\)): data must remain stable for at least this long after the clock edge, for the same reason.

ASCII Timing Diagram — Setup and Hold Windows around a Rising Clock Edge

CLK   ______        ┌──────────────
          |        |
          |________|
                    ^
                    |  (rising edge — flip-flop samples D HERE)
                    |
D     ────╳╳╳╳──────┼──────╳╳╳╳────
          <--------->      <------->
           t_setup            t_hold
      (D must be stable      (D must stay stable
       BEFORE this window)    AFTER the edge)

      |<------ the "keep-out zone" — D must NOT change in here ------>|

If a signal arrives too late (setup violation) or changes too soon after the clock (hold violation), the flip-flop can enter metastability — its output hovers at an invalid voltage between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time before randomly resolving. This is scary precisely because it's probabilistic: a chip might work for a million cycles and randomly fail on the millionth-and-first.

The fundamental timing equation — why a chip has a maximum clock frequency:

\[ T_{clk} \geq t_{cq} + t_{logic} + t_{setup} \]

  • \(t_{clk}\) — the clock period (1/frequency)
  • \(t_{cq}\) — clock-to-Q delay: how long after the clock edge the source flip-flop updates its output
  • \(t_{logic}\) — propagation delay through the combinational logic in between
  • \(t_{setup}\) — the destination flip-flop's setup time requirement

This is what "timing closure" means in VLSI — solving this inequality for every path in a chip with millions of paths.

Clock Skew is the difference in arrival time of the same clock edge at two different flip-flops, from the clock signal traveling different wire lengths. Positive skew helps setup timing but hurts hold timing, and vice versa.

Clock Jitter is cycle-to-cycle variation in when the clock edge actually arrives, from noise in the clock generator (e.g. a PLL). Unlike skew, jitter is random and must be budgeted as a margin that can never be fully eliminated.

1.4 Computer Arithmetic & Number Systems

Two's Complement — how negative numbers physically exist in hardware. Hardware has no concept of a minus sign — only wires that are HIGH or LOW. Two's complement lets the same adder circuit handle positive and negative numbers identically. To negate a number: invert every bit, then add 1.

Example, 8-bit representation of +5 and -5:

 +5  =  0000 0101
        1111 1010   (invert every bit — "ones' complement")
      +          1
        ─────────────
 -5  =  1111 1011

Why this is brilliant — adding 5 + (-5) with ordinary binary addition:

   0000 0101
 + 1111 1011
 ────────────
 1 0000 0000   → 8-bit result is 0000 0000, carry-out discarded → correctly = 0

The same adder circuit that adds positive numbers also correctly adds negative ones — no special "subtractor" hardware needed. This is why two's complement is universal in digital hardware.

Fixed-Point vs Floating-Point

Fixed-point: the binary point sits at a fixed, implicit position. A Q8.8 format has 8 integer + 8 fractional bits — 0000 0001.1000 0000 represents 1.5. Simple, fast, exact for its resolution, but fixed range and precision — exactly why microcontrollers without a hardware FPU often use fixed-point math for speed.

Floating-point (IEEE 754): the binary point floats — sign, exponent, and mantissa, like scientific notation. A 32-bit float:

 [ 1 bit sign ][ 8 bits exponent ][ 23 bits mantissa ]
   31           30............23   22..................0

 Value = (-1)^sign × 1.mantissa × 2^(exponent - 127)
                                          ^
                                    "bias" of 127

Floating-point gives enormous range (~\(10^{-38}\) to ~\(10^{38}\)) at the cost of variable precision (numbers far from zero are less precise — why comparing floats with == is dangerous) and dedicated FPU hardware needs. This exact trade-off is why, in Section 6, quantizing a neural network from FP32 to INT8 is such a big deal for microcontrollers — INT8 math is dramatically cheaper in silicon area, power, and speed than FP32.

2 · Digital VLSI & Silicon Architecture

This is the section where you stop reading about transistors and start building with them — not physically (that needs a billion-dollar fab), but through Hardware Description Languages (HDLs), which let you describe circuit behavior in text and simulate it exactly as real silicon would behave. Every example below is something you can run for free right now in a tool like EDA Playground or by installing Icarus Verilog (iverilog) + GTKWave on your own laptop.

2.1 Hardware Description Languages: Verilog & SystemVerilog Paradigms

The single most important mental shift when learning Verilog: you are not writing a sequential program. You are describing a physical circuit that all exists and runs at the same time. Every always block you write is a separate piece of hardware, and all of them are active simultaneously, every instant, forever — there is no "line 1 runs before line 2" the way there is in C.

Combinational logic — a circuit with NO memory, where the output depends only on the current inputs (like a NAND gate, an adder, or a multiplexer):

// A 4-bit combinational adder — pure "wires and gates", no clock involved
module adder4 (
    input  [3:0] a,
    input  [3:0] b,
    output [4:0] sum   // 5 bits to hold the carry-out
);
    assign sum = a + b;   // 'assign' = continuous, combinational logic
endmodule

Sequential logic — a circuit WITH memory, where the output depends on current inputs and what happened before, captured on clock edges:

// A simple 8-bit up-counter with synchronous reset
module counter8 (
    input        clk,
    input        rst_n,   // active-low reset
    output reg [7:0] count
);
    always @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n)
            count <= 8'd0;      // reset to zero
        else
            count <= count + 1; // increment every clock edge
    end
endmodule

Blocking (=) vs Non-Blocking (<=) — the #1 beginner bug in all of Verilog

  • = (blocking) executes immediately, in program order, inside a single always block — use this only inside combinational always @(*) blocks.
  • <= (non-blocking) schedules the update for the end of the current time step, so all right-hand sides use old values before anything updates — use this always inside clocked always @(posedge clk) blocks.

Why it matters — a 2-stage shift register meant to delay a signal by two clock cycles:

// CORRECT — non-blocking assignments
always @(posedge clk) begin
    q1 <= d;    // q1 gets the OLD value of d
    q2 <= q1;   // q2 gets the OLD value of q1 (not the new one just computed above!)
end
// Result: q2 always lags q1 by one cycle, q1 lags d by one cycle —
// the correct behavior of two real, physical flip-flops in series.
// WRONG — blocking assignments in sequential logic
always @(posedge clk) begin
    q1 = d;     // q1 updates immediately
    q2 = q1;    // q2 immediately copies the NEW q1 (same cycle!)
end
// Result: q2 = d in the SAME clock edge — you've accidentally built ONE
// flip-flop of delay instead of TWO. In real silicon this is physically
// impossible; the simulator is quietly lying to you about the hardware.
Rule of thumb: combinational block → blocking =. Clocked block → non-blocking <=. Mixing them incorrectly is the #1 reason a design simulates fine but synthesizes into completely different (or broken) real hardware.

2.2 Finite State Machine (FSM) Design: Mealy vs Moore

An FSM is how you give a circuit "memory of context" — the same input can produce different behavior depending on what state the circuit is currently in. Almost every real digital system (a UART controller, a traffic light, a vending machine, a CPU's control unit) is fundamentally an FSM.

  • Moore machine: output depends only on the current state. Outputs are stable, changing only on clock edges — generally safer and easier to reason about.
  • Mealy machine: output depends on current state AND current input. Can react within the same clock cycle — often fewer states, but outputs can glitch on noisy inputs.

Example: a sequence detector that outputs 1 when it sees the bit pattern 1011 on a serial input line (overlapping matches allowed) — Moore-style, output tied purely to state:

module seq_detector_moore (
    input      clk,
    input      rst_n,
    input      din,
    output reg dout
);
    // State encoding
    localparam S0=3'd0, S1=3'd1, S2=3'd2, S3=3'd3, S_MATCH=3'd4;
    reg [2:0] state, next_state;

    // Sequential: state register (the ONLY clocked block)
    always @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) state <= S0;
        else        state <= next_state;
    end

    // Combinational: next-state logic
    always @(*) begin
        case (state)
            S0:      next_state = din ? S1 : S0;
            S1:      next_state = din ? S1 : S2;      // saw "1"
            S2:      next_state = din ? S3 : S0;      // saw "10"
            S3:      next_state = din ? S_MATCH : S2; // saw "101"
            S_MATCH: next_state = din ? S1 : S2;       // saw "1011" -> match!
            default: next_state = S0;
        endcase
    end

    // Combinational: Moore output logic — depends ONLY on 'state'
    always @(*) begin
        dout = (state == S_MATCH);
    end
endmodule

Notice the three-always-block Moore FSM template: (1) state register, (2) next-state logic, (3) output logic. This template will get you through 90% of FSM design problems in coursework and interviews. A Mealy version would compute dout combinationally from (state, din) together — it can detect the match one cycle "earlier" but becomes vulnerable to glitches on din.

2.3 Computer Architecture: The 5-Stage RISC-V Pipeline

A single instruction doesn't execute instantly — it moves through distinct stages, and a pipelined CPU overlaps multiple instructions at different stages simultaneously, the same way a car factory assembly line has multiple cars in different stages of assembly at once.

 Classic 5-Stage RISC-V Pipeline

  IF  →  ID  →  EX  →  MEM  →  WB
  |       |      |       |      |
Fetch   Decode  Execute Memory Write
instr   & read  (ALU)   access Back
from    regs                  (result
memory                        to reg
                               file)

 Timeline — 4 instructions overlapping (each row = 1 instruction, each column = 1 cycle):

           Cycle:  1    2    3    4    5    6    7    8
 Instr 1 (I1):     IF   ID   EX   MEM  WB
 Instr 2 (I2):          IF   ID   EX   MEM  WB
 Instr 3 (I3):               IF   ID   EX   MEM  WB
 Instr 4 (I4):                    IF   ID   EX   MEM  WB

 Without pipelining: 4 × 5 = 20 cycles. With pipelining (ideal, no hazards): 8 cycles — near-4x speedup.

Stage responsibilities:

  • IF — read the next instruction from instruction memory at the Program Counter (PC) address.
  • ID — decode the opcode, read source operands from the register file.
  • EX — the ALU performs the computation (add, subtract, compare, compute a memory address).
  • MEM — for loads/stores, read from or write to data memory; other instructions pass through.
  • WB — write the final result back into the register file.

Hazards — when the pipeline's overlapping breaks down:

HazardWhat happensAnalogyFix
StructuralTwo instructions need the same hardware resource in the same cycleTwo cars need the same paint booth at onceDuplicate the resource (Harvard architecture), or stall
DataAn instruction needs a value a prior, still-in-flight instruction hasn't written back yetNext car needs a part the previous station hasn't finished installingForwarding/bypassing the ALU result directly to the next EX stage; a stall/bubble when forwarding can't cover it
ControlA branch's outcome isn't known until EX, but IF already had to guess what to fetch nextNot knowing which fork the road takes until halfway down one pathBranch prediction + pipeline flush on misprediction

Modern high-performance cores push this further — deeper pipelines (10-20+ stages), out-of-order execution, branch predictors right over 95% of the time — but all built from these same three hazard categories at the core.

2.4 Synthesis & Physical Design Flow: RTL → Tape-out

This is the industrial pipeline that turns the Verilog you write into an actual physical chip — understanding it is what separates "I can write Verilog" from "I understand VLSI as a career":

  RTL Design           Your Verilog/SystemVerilog code (what you write)
       ↓
  Functional Sim       Verify logical correctness (iverilog, VCS, QuestaSim)
       ↓
  Logic Synthesis       Tool (Synopsys Design Compiler, Cadence Genus) converts
                         RTL into a "gate-level netlist" — real standard-cell
                         gates (AND2, NAND3, DFF...) from a vendor cell library,
                         optimized for area / speed / power
       ↓
  Static Timing         STA tool checks EVERY path against the t_setup / t_hold
  Analysis (STA)         equations from Section 1.3 — "timing closure" means
                         every single path passes
       ↓
  Place & Route (P&R)   Tool decides the PHYSICAL x,y location of every gate,
                         then draws the actual metal wires connecting them —
                         where clock skew and real physical delay get finalized
       ↓
  Physical Verification  DRC (obeys the fab's manufacturing rules?) and
                         LVS (does the layout match the intended circuit?)
       ↓
  Tape-out               Final verified design sent to the fab (TSMC, Samsung,
                         GlobalFoundries) as a GDSII file — the manufacturing
                         "recipe" for the physical wafers

Each arrow above is, in a real VLSI job, often an entire specialized role — synthesis engineer, STA/timing engineer, physical design (P&R) engineer, DFT engineer. Worth remembering for Section 8: a VLSI career is rarely "design the whole chip alone" — it's usually deep specialization in one or two of these stages.

3 · Embedded Systems & ARM Cortex Microarchitectures

This is the section where things start blinking, spinning, and beeping on your own desk. Everything here is written as bare-metal code — no vendor HAL, no Arduino digitalWrite(). You write directly to the same memory-mapped registers the HAL uses internally. This is slower to write but it's the only way to actually understand what a microcontroller is doing — exactly the skill that separates "I can use STM32CubeIDE's generated code" from "I can debug a system when the generated code breaks."

Note on your ESP32: ESP32 uses a Xtensa (or, in newer variants, RISC-V) core, not ARM Cortex-M. The concepts below — memory-mapped registers, interrupt controllers, volatile pointers, bit-masking — transfer directly. Only exact register names/addresses differ. This section uses ARM Cortex-M as the reference architecture because it's the most widely taught and documented; you'll find the equivalent ESP32 register map in Espressif's Technical Reference Manual using the same reading skills built here.

3.1 ARM Cortex-M Architecture

The Memory Map — how a Cortex-M "sees" everything as an address. Peripherals (GPIO, timers, UART...) aren't accessed through special instructions — they're mapped into the same address space as regular RAM. Writing to a GPIO register is, to the CPU, indistinguishable from writing to a normal variable in RAM — it's just a store instruction to a specific address.

 Simplified Cortex-M Memory Map (addresses vary slightly by exact chip)

 0xFFFFFFFF  ┌─────────────────────────┐
             │   Vendor-Specific /      │
             │   System Peripherals     │
 0xE0000000  ├─────────────────────────┤  ← NVIC, SysTick, debug registers live here
             │   External Device        │
 0xA0000000  ├─────────────────────────┤
             │   External RAM           │
 0x60000000  ├─────────────────────────┤
             │   Peripherals (GPIO,      │
             │   Timers, UART, SPI, I2C) │  ← memory-mapped registers you'll write to
 0x40000000  ├─────────────────────────┤
             │   SRAM (your variables,   │
             │   stack, heap)            │
 0x20000000  ├─────────────────────────┤
             │   Code (Flash — where      │
             │   your compiled program    │
             │   physically lives)         │
 0x00000000  └─────────────────────────┘

NVIC — the Nested Vectored Interrupt Controller. This hardware block decides which interrupt handler runs when multiple things need CPU attention at once. Key registers you'll touch directly in bare-metal code:

  • NVIC_ISER (Interrupt Set-Enable) — write a 1 to a bit to enable that interrupt
  • NVIC_ICER (Interrupt Clear-Enable) — write a 1 to disable it
  • NVIC_IPR (Interrupt Priority) — set priority (lower number = higher priority, confusingly)
  • The NVIC also supports nesting: a higher-priority interrupt can itself interrupt a currently-running lower-priority handler — the "nested" in the name.

MSP vs PSP — two stack pointers, one CPU. Cortex-M has two physical stack pointer registers, though only one is "active" (mapped to R13) at any moment:

  • MSP (Main Stack Pointer): used by default at reset, and always used while running any interrupt/exception handler.
  • PSP (Process Stack Pointer): optionally used by application/thread-mode code — exactly the mechanism an RTOS (Section 5) uses to give every task its own private stack while the kernel and interrupts share the MSP.

Register set: Cortex-M has 16 visible registers: R0-R12 general-purpose, R13 the active Stack Pointer, R14 the Link Register (LR) holding the function-call return address, and R15 the Program Counter (PC). The PSR splits into APSR (flags: Negative, Zero, Carry, oVerflow), IPSR (currently-executing exception number), and EPSR (execution state bits) — often accessed together as xPSR.

3.2 Bare-Metal C Engineering

The volatile keyword — the single most important C keyword in embedded systems. A compiler is normally free to optimize away repeated reads of a variable if it "sees" nothing in the C code changing it. But a hardware register's value can change outside the compiler's view — a UART status bit flips because a byte physically arrived over the wire, not because any C statement wrote to it. volatile tells the compiler: never assume you know this value — always read/write it from real memory, every time, exactly as written.

// WITHOUT volatile — the compiler might optimize this into an infinite loop
// that never actually re-reads the register:
#define STATUS_REG (*(unsigned int*)0x40011000)
while ((STATUS_REG & 0x1) == 0) { }   // BUG RISK: may never re-read hardware!

// WITH volatile — forces a genuine memory read on every loop iteration:
#define STATUS_REG (*(volatile unsigned int*)0x40011000)
while ((STATUS_REG & 0x1) == 0) { }   // CORRECT — guaranteed fresh read every time

Bit-masking — touching ONE bit in a 32-bit register without disturbing the other 31

#define BIT(n)   (1U << (n))

reg |=  BIT(5);        // SET bit 5 to 1, leave every other bit untouched
reg &= ~BIT(5);        // CLEAR bit 5 to 0, leave every other bit untouched
reg ^=  BIT(5);        // TOGGLE bit 5, leave every other bit untouched
if (reg & BIT(5)) { }  // READ/TEST bit 5 without modifying the register
  • | (OR) — set bits (1 in the mask forces that bit to 1; 0 leaves it unchanged)
  • & with inverted mask (~) — clear bits (0 in the mask forces that bit to 0; 1 leaves it unchanged)
  • ^ (XOR) — toggle bits marked 1 in the mask
  • << / >> — shift a mask into position, e.g. BIT(5) is really 1 << 5

Memory-mapped register access with structs — the professional pattern

Define a C struct whose layout exactly matches the peripheral's register layout, then overlay it onto the correct base address with a pointer cast:

// Simplified GPIO peripheral register layout (order MUST match the datasheet exactly)
typedef struct {
    volatile uint32_t MODER;     // 0x00 — mode register (input/output/alt-fn/analog)
    volatile uint32_t OTYPER;    // 0x04 — output type (push-pull / open-drain)
    volatile uint32_t OSPEEDR;   // 0x08 — output speed
    volatile uint32_t PUPDR;     // 0x0C — pull-up/pull-down config
    volatile uint32_t IDR;       // 0x10 — input data register (read pin states)
    volatile uint32_t ODR;       // 0x14 — output data register (write pin states)
    volatile uint32_t BSRR;      // 0x18 — bit set/reset register (atomic pin control)
} GPIO_TypeDef;

#define GPIOA_BASE   0x40020000UL
#define GPIOA        ((GPIO_TypeDef *) GPIOA_BASE)

// Now register access reads like clean, self-documenting C:
GPIOA->MODER |= (1U << (5 * 2));   // set pin 5 to output mode (2 bits/pin in MODER)
GPIOA->ODR   |= (1U << 5);          // drive pin 5 HIGH

3.3 Complete Register-Level Driver: Bare-Metal GPIO + Timer + Interrupt (No Vendor HAL)

This example blinks an LED using a hardware timer interrupt instead of a busy-wait delay() — the correct embedded pattern, since it doesn't block the CPU from doing anything else while "waiting."

#include <stdint.h>

/* ---- Minimal register map (STM32F4-style addresses, illustrative) ---- */
#define RCC_AHB1ENR   (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40023830)  // clock enable register
#define GPIOA_MODER   (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40020000)
#define GPIOA_ODR     (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40020014)

#define TIM2_CR1      (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40000000)  // control register
#define TIM2_DIER     (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x4000000C)  // DMA/interrupt enable reg
#define TIM2_SR       (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40000010)  // status register
#define TIM2_PSC      (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x40000028)  // prescaler
#define TIM2_ARR      (*(volatile uint32_t*)0x4000002C)  // auto-reload value

#define NVIC_ISER0    (*(volatile uint32_t*)0xE000E100)  // interrupt enable

volatile uint8_t led_state = 0;

/* ---- Step 1: enable peripheral clocks ---- */
void clocks_init(void) {
    RCC_AHB1ENR |= (1U << 0);   // enable GPIOA clock (bit 0 = GPIOA per datasheet)
    RCC_AHB1ENR |= (1U << 0);   // (TIM2 clock enable would be a separate APB register)
}

/* ---- Step 2: configure PA5 as a general-purpose output (the LED pin) ---- */
void gpio_init(void) {
    GPIOA_MODER &= ~(3U << (5 * 2));  // clear the 2 mode bits for pin 5
    GPIOA_MODER |=  (1U << (5 * 2));  // set to '01' = general-purpose output
}

/* ---- Step 3: configure TIM2 to overflow (and interrupt) every ~500ms ---- */
void timer_init(void) {
    TIM2_PSC = 16000 - 1;    // assuming 16MHz clock -> prescaler gives 1kHz tick
    TIM2_ARR = 500 - 1;      // count 500 ticks -> interrupt every 500ms
    TIM2_DIER |= (1U << 0);  // enable Update Interrupt (UIE bit)
    TIM2_CR1  |= (1U << 0);  // enable the counter (CEN bit)
    NVIC_ISER0 |= (1U << 28); // enable TIM2's interrupt line in the NVIC (IRQ# 28 here)
}

/* ---- Step 4: the Interrupt Service Routine — this function name must exactly
   match the vector table entry so the hardware jumps here automatically ---- */
void TIM2_IRQHandler(void) {
    if (TIM2_SR & (1U << 0)) {        // check the Update Interrupt Flag (UIF)
        TIM2_SR &= ~(1U << 0);        // MUST manually clear the flag, or fires forever
        led_state ^= 1;               // toggle our tracked state
        if (led_state)
            GPIOA_ODR |= (1U << 5);   // drive PA5 HIGH
        else
            GPIOA_ODR &= ~(1U << 5); // drive PA5 LOW
    }
}

int main(void) {
    clocks_init();
    gpio_init();
    timer_init();

    while (1) {
        // Main loop is completely free — the LED blinks entirely via interrupt,
        // with zero CPU cycles wasted on a busy-wait delay loop. This is the
        // core discipline of real embedded firmware: let hardware do the
        // waiting, and keep the CPU free for real work.
    }
}

Three details worth internalizing, because they show up in every real embedded driver you'll write: (1) clocks must be explicitly enabled before a peripheral does anything — an un-clocked peripheral is electrically "frozen"; (2) the interrupt flag (TIM2_SR here) must be manually cleared inside the ISR, or the same interrupt re-fires the instant you exit — an "interrupt storm"; (3) the ISR function name isn't arbitrary — it must match the name the linker script/vector table expects, since hardware jumps to that vector by address, not by any C-level call mechanism.

4 · Low-Level Communication Protocols Architecture

Every sensor, display, and radio module you'll ever wire to a microcontroller talks over one of these four protocols. Understanding them at the signal-line level — not just "which library function to call" — is what lets you debug a real hardware problem with a logic analyzer instead of guessing.

4.1 UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter)

The defining feature: no shared clock line. Both sides must independently agree on a baud rate (bits per second) in advance and trust their own internal clocks to stay synchronized for the duration of one frame — this is what "asynchronous" means.

Signal lines: just two, plus ground — TX (transmit) and RX (receive), cross-connected between two devices (device A's TX → device B's RX, and vice versa).

     └───┘   └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──────────────────────┘

 Line idles HIGH. A falling edge signals the START bit (always 0),
 which is how the receiver knows exactly when to begin sampling.
  • Start bit: always 0 (LOW) — this transition is what the receiver's clock synchronizes to.
  • Data bits: typically 8, sent LSB-first.
  • Parity bit (optional): simple error detection — even parity means the total count of 1-bits (data+parity) is even; odd parity means odd. Catches single-bit errors only.
  • Stop bit(s): 1 or 2 bits, always HIGH — guarantees the line returns to idle before the next frame.

Common baud rates: 9600, 19200, 115200 bps standard; going faster increases sensitivity to clock drift, since there's no shared clock to correct for it — exactly why UART tops out at a few Mbps, far slower than SPI.

Error checking: parity bit only (weak — misses any even number of bit flips), plus framing errors (receiver expects a HIGH stop bit and sees LOW, meaning bit sync was lost).

4.2 SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface)

The defining feature: a shared clock, full-duplex, and no built-in addressing. SPI is the fastest of the four (routinely 10-50+ MHz), because a dedicated clock line means both sides always know exactly when to sample — no baud-rate guessing.

Signal lines (4 total):

  • SCLK — generated by the Master, tells every device exactly when to read/write a bit.
  • MOSI (Master Out, Slave In) — data flowing master → slave.
  • MISO (Master In, Slave Out) — data flowing slave → master.
  • CS/SS — one per slave device, active-low; the master pulls a specific slave's CS LOW to "address" it. This is SPI's substitute for I2C-style addressing — and its main scalability weakness, since N slaves need N separate CS pins.

Full-duplex means simultaneous, not just bidirectional: on every clock pulse, the master shifts one bit OUT on MOSI and simultaneously shifts one bit IN on MISO — in the exact same clock cycle, unlike I2C or UART's half-duplex-per-frame behavior.

The 4 SPI Modes — two independent settings:

  • CPOL (Clock Polarity): does the clock idle HIGH (CPOL=1) or LOW (CPOL=0)?
  • CPHA (Clock Phase): is data sampled on the clock's first edge (CPHA=0) or second edge (CPHA=1) after CS goes active?

Error checking: SPI has none built in — no parity, no ACK, no CRC by default. It relies entirely on physical signal integrity; any error checking must be added at the application-protocol level.

4.3 I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit)

The defining feature: only 2 wires for potentially dozens of devices, using built-in addressing. Where SPI trades pin count for speed and simplicity, I2C trades speed for a dramatically simpler wiring topology.

Signal lines (2, both open-drain with external pull-up resistors): SDA (Serial Data), SCL (Serial Clock).

Open-drain matters: any device can pull the line LOW, but no device can drive it HIGH — external pull-ups do that. This is why multiple devices safely share the same two wires without conflict: if two devices write different values simultaneously, the line simply reads LOW (the "wired-AND" behavior) instead of a dangerous short.

START and STOP conditions — how I2C creates frame boundaries without any dedicated start/stop line:

  • START: SDA transitions HIGH→LOW while SCL is HIGH (the only time SDA may change while SCL is high, elsewhere in a normal transfer).
  • STOP: SDA transitions LOW→HIGH while SCL is HIGH.
 I2C START and STOP Conditions

 SCL   ‾‾‾‾‾‾\_____/‾‾‾\_____/‾‾‾ ... ‾‾‾\_____/‾‾‾‾‾‾
 SDA   ‾‾‾\___________________ ... ___________/‾‾‾‾‾‾
           ^                                   ^
        START                                STOP
      (SDA falls while             (SDA rises while
       SCL is still HIGH)           SCL is still HIGH)

Frame structure: START → 7-bit (or 10-bit) slave Address + 1 R/W bit → ACK/NACK → 8 data bits → ACK/NACK → ... (repeat) → STOP.

ACK/NACK: after every byte, the receiver pulls SDA LOW for one clock pulse to ACKnowledge. Leaving SDA HIGH is a NACK — "I didn't get that," "no device at that address," or "stop sending, I'm full."

Arbitration: because SDA is open-drain and wired-AND, multiple masters can attempt to transmit simultaneously — each monitors SDA while transmitting, and if it sees LOW when it tried to drive HIGH, it knows another master won and backs off. This is how I2C supports multi-master configurations safely without a separate arbitration bus.

Speed grades: Standard (100 kHz), Fast (400 kHz), Fast mode+ (1 MHz), High-speed (3.4 MHz) — all considerably slower than SPI, the trade-off for 2-wire, multi-device, built-in-addressing simplicity.

4.4 CAN (Controller Area Network)

The defining feature: differential signaling and priority-based arbitration without a master — built for electrically noisy environments (cars, industrial machines).

Signal lines (2, differential pair): CAN_H, CAN_L.

Differential signaling: instead of measuring one wire against ground (which picks up noise easily), a receiver measures the voltage difference between CAN_H and CAN_L. Electrical noise affects both wires equally (common-mode) and cancels out in the difference — exactly why CAN was designed for automotive environments full of motor and ignition noise.

Dominant vs Recessive bits — the mechanism behind master-less arbitration:

  • Recessive (logical 1): CAN_H and CAN_L sit close together (~2.5V both) — a "weak" state.
  • Dominant (logical 0): CAN_H driven high, CAN_L driven low, creating a clear voltage difference — a "strong" state that physically overrides a recessive bit if both are driven simultaneously.

Frame arbitration: every node with a message ready starts transmitting its message identifier bit-by-bit simultaneously with any other waiting node, while also listening to the bus. If a node sends a recessive (1) bit but reads back dominant (0) — because a lower, higher-priority identifier is also transmitting — it knows it lost arbitration and immediately stops, letting the higher-priority message through uninterrupted, with zero collision and zero retransmission delay. Lower numerical identifier = higher priority — a deliberate design choice baked into the bit encoding.

 CAN Bus Arbitration — 3 nodes transmitting overlapping IDs simultaneously

 Node A ID:  1 0 1 1 0 ...   (loses at bit 3: sends 1, bus reads 0 → backs off)
 Node B ID:  1 0 0 1 1 ...   (WINS — sent all dominant/recessive bits cleanly)
 Node C ID:  1 1 0 0 1 ...   (loses at bit 2: sends 1, bus reads 0 → backs off)

 Bus value:  1 0 0 1 1 ...   (dominant "0" always wins on a shared bus)
                 ^
          Node A and C both see their bit didn't match the bus —
          they immediately stop transmitting; Node B continues uninterrupted.

Error checking: CAN has by far the most robust built-in error detection of the four — a 15-bit CRC per frame, plus bit-stuffing (a bit of opposite polarity auto-inserted after 5 identical bits, to keep receivers synchronized), plus real-time error mechanisms (bit monitoring, ACK checking, form-checking) that can flag a faulty node and force it into a "bus-off" state, protecting the rest of the network. This is why CAN is standard for safety-critical automotive/industrial systems — SPI and I2C have no equivalent self-protection.
